This lecture is about the relationship between a person and an imperfect decision aid. The central question is not whether people should trust AI. It is how a joint system should decide when either contributor can be wrong.

Students should leave able to diagnose overreliance, distinguish cognitive offloading from cognitive surrender, explain why collaboration often fails, and propose designs that make complementary performance more likely.

A decision aid selects what to display, when to display it, and how strongly to frame it. That changes what the person notices and how much independent reasoning they perform.

Humans are not the gold standard

Human judgment is adaptive, contextual, and often inconsistent.

HUMAN
Limited attentionWe cannot inspect everything.
Fatigue and workloadPerformance changes with conditions.
Heuristics and biasShortcuts can help or mislead.
Variable expertisePeople differ across cases and time.
Memory and consistencyWe forget and apply criteria unevenly.
Human judgment contains signal and noiseContext helps, but does not guarantee accuracy

Human-centered design does not require pretending that people are perfect. Decision support exists because human attention, memory, consistency, and statistical reasoning are limited.

Human strengths are conditional. Expertise helps when the person has relevant experience and usable cues. Under time pressure or fatigue, the same person may rely differently.

This is the central bridge from imperfection to collaboration. If human and AI errors overlap completely, combining them adds little. If their information or capabilities differ, the team may recover cases that either one misses alone.

Potential does not guarantee performance. The interaction must reveal disagreement and route the case to the contributor who is more likely to be right.

Vaccaro and colleagues synthesized 106 experimental studies. On average, human-AI combinations performed significantly worse than the better solo performer, with Hedges’ g of minus 0.23 and a 95% confidence interval from minus 0.39 to minus 0.07.

The result does not say collaboration is hopeless. It says that complementarity must be designed and measured. Gains were more likely in content-creation tasks and when humans alone outperformed AI alone.

The relative-performance result is especially important. When the AI was already stronger, adding a human often reduced performance. When humans were stronger, they may have been better able to decide when to use the AI. The study cannot prove that mechanism.

Researchers often distinguish improvement over humans from true complementary team performance. If AI alone scores 80, humans score 65, and the team scores 75, assistance helped people but the workflow still destroyed some of the model’s available performance.

The team metric can include more than accuracy, but the comparison must remain explicit.

Tschandl and colleagues tested several representations of AI support across different expertise levels. Among 155 raters, good multiclass probability support produced a median accuracy gain of 9.5 percentage points. When the top AI prediction was replaced with a random wrong answer, the median change became a 6.3-point loss.

A separate analysis combined judgments over 600 images. Human collectives plus AI reached 81.0% accuracy, compared with 73.7% for human collectives and 76.9% for the CNN. The benchmark shows both complementarity and vulnerability, but it does not establish clinical patient benefit.

Two sources of complementarity

Partners add value when they possess different information or different capabilities.

INFORMATION

They see different evidence.

The model sees thousands of historical patterns. The person sees a new constraint, local context, or unrecorded goal.

Can each partner reveal what the other lacks?
+
CAPABILITY

They process evidence differently.

The model searches consistently at scale. The person reasons about values, exceptions, causal stories, and consequences.

Can the workflow allocate work to the stronger contributor?
Difference creates potentialThe workflow must expose and use the difference

Information asymmetry means partners possess different evidence. Capability asymmetry means they can do different things with available evidence. Hemmer and colleagues identify these as key sources of complementarity.

Designs fail when they hide the model’s evidence, suppress human context, or force one contributor to imitate the other.

A good decision-maker accepts correct advice and rejects incorrect advice.

This matrix makes appropriate reliance observable. Overreliance is not simply high use. It is accepting advice when the system is wrong. Underreliance is rejecting the system when it is right.

Evaluation should therefore include intentionally incorrect recommendations. If a study only shows users correct AI advice, it cannot measure their ability to discriminate.

“I trust the AI” is too broad to guide a decision.

Trust calibration should be specific. A model can be reliable at one task, population, language, or operating condition and unreliable elsewhere.

Reliance should also change with stakes and reversibility. A low-cost draft can tolerate more exploratory use than an irreversible eligibility decision.

Automation bias

People often treat an automated cue as a substitute for searching and checking.

AI recommendation
→
attention narrows
contradictory evidence receives less search

→

through the human
Overreliance can look like agreementThe missing behavior is independent checking

Automation bias describes a tendency to over-rely on automated recommendations, often with reduced vigilance and information search. The system becomes a heuristic for what deserves attention.

This is not limited to novice users or unintelligent people. It can emerge when automation is usually correct, time is scarce, or independent verification is effortful.

Two signatures of automation bias

Automation can make us miss what it omits and do what it wrongly suggests.

OMISSION

The system stays silent.

The person fails to notice or act because no alert appeared.

No warning → hazard goes unexamined
COMMISSION

The system recommends the wrong action.

The person follows the recommendation despite conflicting evidence.

Wrong warning → unnecessary action
Silence can direct attention tooInspect both missed and followed errors

Omission errors occur when users miss a problem because the aid did not flag it. Commission errors occur when users follow an incorrect recommendation.

The design response differs. Omission risk requires maintaining active search and situation awareness. Commission risk requires enabling challenge, comparison, and rejection.

Evidence across domains

Automation bias is common in the literature, but the evidence base has real limitations.

40
studies met review criteria

Human factors and health-care research, 1983–2015

81%25 of 31 studies testing omission errors reported evidence of bias
91%21 of 23 studies testing commission errors reported evidence of bias
9studies tested statistical significance against a manual control
Interpret carefully: Samples were often small and homogeneous, measures varied widely, and most studies did not report an effect size against an unassisted control.
Lyell & Coiera · JAMIA · systematic reviewPrevalence of evidence is not a pooled effect size

Lyell and Coiera screened 890 records and included 40 studies. Most studies that tested omission or commission errors reported automation bias. The review also found that bias could occur in single-task settings when verification was cognitively demanding.

Do not turn the percentages into a single causal effect. Only nine included studies tested statistical significance against a nonautomated control, measures differed substantially, and samples were often small. The strongest defensible conclusion is that the phenomenon recurs across tasks and deserves direct testing in each workflow.

A crucial distinction

Cognitive offloading can help. Cognitive surrender gives up the judgment itself.

OFFLOADING

Move part of the cognitive work into an external tool.

Use a calculator, map, checklist, search tool, or AI draft.
The person still frames, evaluates, and owns the decision.
VS
SURRENDER

Adopt the external answer with little independent scrutiny.

The system supplies the frame, conclusion, and stopping point.
The person cannot explain, test, or recover the reasoning.
External cognition is not inherently harmfulThe issue is preserved agency and understanding

Cognitive offloading is a well-established part of human cognition. We use tools and external representations to reduce cognitive demand. It can improve performance and free attention for other work.

Cognitive surrender is an emerging label rather than a settled clinical construct. Here it means giving up independent framing, scrutiny, or ownership because the AI output prematurely closes the problem.

Cognitive surrender

The danger is not that the AI thinks. It is that the person stops deciding how to think.

“

An emerging term for adopting AI output with minimal scrutiny while intuition, deliberation, or verification is bypassed.

Useful as a diagnostic idea. Not a medical diagnosis and not every instance of AI assistance.
Watch for premature closureFrictionless answers can hide lost agency

Use the term carefully. The 2026 cognitive surrender theory proposes a third, external artificial reasoning source that can override or replace intuitive and deliberative processing. The term is new and the broader evidence base is still developing.

The practical value is to ask whether the person remains epistemically active: Did they define the problem, inspect the evidence, consider alternatives, and retain responsibility for the decision?

Separate the evidence from the label

The mechanisms are better established than the term “cognitive surrender.”

ESTABLISHED

GROWING

This slide is deliberately epistemic. Cognitive offloading has a long research history. Automation bias and passive reliance have experimental support across multiple tasks. Cognitive surrender is a new theoretical label that attempts to integrate related mechanisms.

The label may be useful for asking whether a person remains epistemically active, but it should not be presented as a validated psychological scale, clinical condition, or universal consequence of AI use.

Five warning signs

You may have surrendered when the answer survives but your understanding disappears.

1

You cannot explain why the recommendation fits this case.

2

You did not form a view before seeing the output.

3

You searched for confirmation, not contradiction.

4

You cannot continue if the system becomes unavailable.

5

You still own the consequences but no longer feel ownership of the reasoning.

A correct answer can still weaken capabilityEvaluate process as well as outcome

These warning signs are practical diagnostics derived from the literature on cognitive offloading, automation bias, self-efficacy, and cognitive surrender. They are not a validated scale.

A user can reach the correct answer through surrender. That makes accuracy alone insufficient for evaluating learning, expertise maintenance, or long-term resilience.

Participants were randomly assigned to no AI, copy-and-paste AI, or human-first collaboration. The outcomes were self-efficacy for doing similar work without AI, psychological ownership, and work meaningfulness.

Passive use reduced all three outcomes. Self-efficacy and meaningfulness remained lower when participants then completed a similar task manually. Bainbridge identified this problem in 1983. Highly automated systems still need people for abnormal conditions, yet automation deprives those people of routine practice and situation awareness.

Generative systems create a related problem when users become editors of completed outputs instead of active problem solvers. Rare failure then demands skills the workflow no longer exercises.

Bansal et al. · CHI · 2021Acceptance is not the same as appropriate reliance

Bansal and colleagues ran large-scale experiments with 1,626 participants across three tasks. They deliberately selected examples so AI and human accuracy were comparable, leaving room for complementarity.

Teams achieved complementary performance in the study conditions, but explanations did not significantly outperform a confidence-only baseline. Explanations increased accuracy when the AI was right and decreased it when the AI was wrong, producing little net improvement. The result is not that explanations never help. It is that persuasive acceptance is not the same as calibrated error detection.

Across four preregistered experiments with 3,800 participants, the researchers varied model transparency and feature count while keeping the displayed predictions functionally identical.

A clear two-feature model was easier to simulate, but did not improve beneficial reliance. In some experiments, clear models made participants worse at correcting sizable errors on unusual apartments. In Experiment 4, an explicit outlier-focus message improved correction for both unusual cases. The design lesson is to support the concrete behavior needed, not assume general transparency will create it.

The study compared three cognitive forcing functions with two common explainable-AI interfaces and a no-AI baseline. The simulated AI was 75% accurate.

Forcing functions improved rejection of wrong AI advice, which is the precise behavior we want to protect. Socratic test

A team agrees with its AI 95% of the time. Is that evidence of success?

1How often is the AI wrong, and on which cases?

2When it is wrong, how often do people catch it?

3Do users form an independent view before seeing advice?

4Can they explain and reverse the final decision?

5Does disagreement improve the system or get punished?

Agreement is ambiguous. It can indicate shared competence, shared bias, or cognitive surrender.

Give students a minute to answer before revealing the bottom line. Agreement may be desirable when the AI is right, but the same rate can conceal total overreliance if users also follow every wrong recommendation.

This question ties together the behavioral definition of reliance, the need for error cases in evaluation, and the danger of optimizing acceptance.

A six-question diagnostic

Design the partnership around disagreement, not obedience.

Decision: What is being predicted, recommended, or executed?

Difference: What unique information or capability does each partner hold?

Reliance: Can the person accept correct advice and reject incorrect advice?

Agency: Are they still framing, evaluating, and owning the judgment?

Recovery: Can the team detect, correct, undo, and learn from failure?

Evidence: Does the joint system outperform both partners on the outcomes that matter?
