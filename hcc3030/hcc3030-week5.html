The first misconception

A privacy failure can happen even when security works perfectly.

✓

Encrypted in transit

No attacker entered the system.

Access controls worked

Only authorized employees and services used the data.

The use was still wrong

Data collected for one purpose shaped a decision people did not expect or accept.

Security asks whether use was authorized. Privacy also asks whether the authorized use was appropriate.
Confidentiality is one privacy controlIt is not the whole of privacy

Security protects confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Privacy concerns the effects of processing data about people, including intended processing by authorized actors.

Encryption cannot decide whether a new use is legitimate, whether an inference should exist, or whether a person should be able to contest an outcome.

Two forms of human agency

Privacy governs information flows. Contestability governs institutional response.

PRIVACY

Before and during processing

Can people reasonably understand, influence, limit, or avoid how information about them is collected, inferred, used, retained, and shared?

CONTESTABILITY

When a system or decision is disputed

Can people see what happened, correct the record, present relevant evidence, obtain review, and receive a remedy?

Both require usable organizational processesNeither is solved by a disclosure alone

This is the organizing distinction for the lecture. Privacy manages data processing and its effects. Contestability makes systems open and responsive to dispute.

The concepts overlap. A person may need data access to challenge a decision, and contestation may reveal a privacy violation.

Nissenbaum · contextual integrityPrivacy is not simply public versus secret

Contextual integrity evaluates an information flow through the actors, attributes, and transmission principles that define the social context. Information can be known in one context and still be inappropriate to move into another.

This framework helps explain why a long privacy policy is insufficient. The central issue is whether the flow fits legitimate contextual norms.

Purpose limitation

Data collected to provide a service should not quietly become fuel for another business goal.

What was examined

Online counseling service

The FTC examined BetterHelp’s promises and its disclosures of email addresses, IP addresses, and health-questionnaire information.

What was found
$7.8M

The final order prohibited sharing health data for advertising and required deletion, consent, retention limits, and a privacy program.

What it does not prove

The order resolved allegations and does not establish that every health app follows the same practices. Health-app coverage also depends on the applicable law and entity.

Federal Trade Commission · BetterHelp · 2023Service data became advertising data

The FTC alleged that BetterHelp shared sensitive consumer information with advertising platforms despite promising limited uses. Empirical case · Platform surveillance

The FTC found extensive collection, retention, sharing, and weak minimization across major platforms.

What was examined
9 services

FTC compulsory-process orders examined major social-media and video-streaming companies and their data practices.

What was found

Vast surveillance

The report described broad collection on and off platforms, extensive monetization, long or indefinite retention, and inadequate protections for children and teens.

What it does not prove

The report describes the practices of the examined companies during the covered period. It is not a measurement of every platform or current product version.

Federal Trade Commission · 2024Collection scale changes organizational power

The FTC’s 2024 staff report drew on orders issued to nine major services. It described extensive collection, combination, retention, dissemination, and monetization of personal data, including data gathered beyond the companies’ own services.

The report is an institutional investigation, not a controlled experiment. Use it as evidence about observed business practices and governance gaps.

Privacy and security overlap, but they are not synonyms

Security protects the system. Privacy protects people from data processing.

SECURITY FAILURE

An unauthorized actor steals prompt logs.

A database is altered or disabled.

A credential exposes a model endpoint.

BOTH

PRIVACY FAILURE

An authorized team reuses prompts for an unrelated purpose.

A model infers a sensitive trait.

A correct profile is used to manipulate or exclude.

Some incidents are bothRisk assessment must cover intended processing

A breach can be both a security and privacy failure. But privacy risk can arise when the system works as designed.

This distinction keeps teams from treating compliance with access-control and encryption requirements as proof that the data practice is appropriate.

Models create new privacy attack surfaces

An attacker may learn about training data without seeing the dataset.

MEMBERSHIP

Was this record used?

Infer whether a person or example was part of model training.

INVERSION

What did the model learn?

Use outputs or confidence to reconstruct sensitive features or prototypes.

EXTRACTION

Can the model repeat it?

Query the model until memorized training examples appear.

The model can become an information channelRisk depends on access, overfitting, and memorization

Membership inference, model inversion, and training-data extraction are distinct. They require different access assumptions and reveal different information.

These are attack classes, not proof that every model leaks useful personal data. Privacy evaluation should state the adversary’s knowledge, query access, and success criterion.

Empirical case · GPT-2 training-data extraction

Researchers recovered hundreds of verbatim training sequences by querying a language model.

What was examined

Black-box generation attack

Carlini and colleagues sampled GPT-2 outputs, ranked likely memorization, and verified matches against training data.

What was found
Hundreds

Recovered sequences included names, phone numbers, email addresses, code, chat logs, and UUIDs, even when a sequence appeared in one document.

What it does not prove

The attack targeted GPT-2 and a known training corpus. Extractability depends on model, data duplication, size, prompting, and access.

Carlini et al. · USENIX Security · 2021Publicly scraped does not mean harmless to reproduce

The study demonstrated training-data extraction against GPT-2 and recovered hundreds of verbatim sequences, including public personally identifying information. Larger models in the study were more vulnerable than smaller ones.

The authors evaluated a particular model family and corpus. Do not generalize the recovery rate to every current model.

[Sources]
Carlini et al., Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/carlini-extracting

Empirical case · Production language models

Alignment reduced obvious leakage but did not eliminate memorization.

What was examined

Open, semi-open, and closed models

Nasr and colleagues scaled extraction attacks across Pythia, GPT-Neo, LLaMA, Falcon, and ChatGPT.

What was found
150×

A divergence attack made aligned ChatGPT emit training data at a rate 150 times higher than normal chatbot behavior in their experiment.

What it does not prove

This was an adversarial research setup from 2023. Providers, models, defenses, interfaces, and training data have changed.

Nasr et al. · 2023Behavioral alignment is not a privacy guarantee

The paper showed scalable extraction across multiple access regimes. For ChatGPT, the authors developed a divergence attack that increased training-data emission relative to normal behavior by a factor of 150.

The result does not mean ordinary use reveals data at that rate. It demonstrates that surface behavior can hide underlying memorization and that privacy testing needs adversarial evaluation.

Nasr et al., Scalable Extraction of Training Data from Production Language Models: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035

Derived representations are still sensitive

Embeddings, summaries, and logs are not privacy-free substitutes for source data.

EMBEDDING

May preserve semantic attributes and enable similarity searches the user never anticipated.

SUMMARY

May turn uncertain or contextual text into a durable claim about a person.

LOG

May collect prompts, outputs, tool calls, identifiers, errors, and operational metadata.

FEEDBACK

May move production conversations into annotation or model-improvement workflows.

Derived data require classification and retention rulesTransformation does not erase provenance

An embedding is a derived representation, not automatic anonymization. A summary can amplify an error by making it easier to reuse. Logs may become the broadest data collection layer because they exist for debugging and quality control.

Apply access, retention, deletion, and purpose rules to derived data as well as raw inputs.

Three privacy engineering objectives

A privacy-preserving system should be understandable, manageable, and hard to link unnecessarily.

PREDICTABILITY

Can people and operators form reliable expectations?

Data practices behave as described across the lifecycle.

MANAGEABILITY

Can data and processing be changed?

Access, correct, delete, withdraw, restrict, and review.

DISASSOCIABILITY

Can processing avoid unnecessary identity linkage?

Use data without tying every operation to a person or device.

NISTIR 8062Engineering objectives, not interface slogans

NISTIR 8062 defines predictability, manageability, and disassociability as privacy engineering objectives. They translate broad principles into system capabilities.

A design may prioritize the objectives differently by context. The value is that teams can test whether the system actually supports them.

Control 1 · Reduce the data surface

The safest sensitive record is often the one the system never collected or already deleted.

Collect less

Remove fields, telemetry, precision, or history that the task does not need.

Keep it briefly

Set deletion schedules for prompts, raw files, logs, embeddings, and backups.

Process locally

Keep data on device or within a controlled boundary when central collection adds little value.

For each data element: what decision requires it, and for how long?
Minimization shrinks every later threatRetention is a product decision

Data minimization reduces exposure to breach, misuse, subpoena, insider access, repurposing, and future linkage. It also forces clarity about the task.

Evaluate each data element and every retained derivative. Deleting a source file while retaining an embedding, summary, or model-improvement copy may not satisfy the intended control.

Control 2 · Constrain the flow

Separate data, identities, purposes, and authority.

IDENTITYKeep identity keys apart from analytic data where possible.
PURPOSEUse separate stores or pipelines for service, safety, analytics, and training.
ACCESSGrant the minimum role, record every access, and review unusual use.
OUTPUTLimit query detail, bulk export, confidence, and repeated probing.
Access control should follow the decision, not the organizational chart.
Compartmentalization limits blast radiusLogging creates accountability only if reviewed

Separation reduces the ability to link, reuse, or extract data. Output controls matter because an attacker may learn through repeated model queries even without database access.

Logs are useful for accountability, but they also create sensitive data and need their own access and retention controls.

Control 3 · Privacy-enhancing technologies

Different technical tools protect against different disclosures.

DIFFERENTIAL PRIVACY

Add calibrated randomness so population statistics reveal less about any one person.

Best for aggregate analysis and some learning settings.
FEDERATED LEARNING

Move training toward devices or local data stores instead of centralizing raw records.

Updates can still leak without added protections.
SYNTHETIC DATA

Generate artificial records that resemble patterns in real data.

May preserve bias or memorize rare examples.
ENCRYPTED COMPUTATION

Compute while data are protected through specialized cryptographic techniques.

Can add cost, latency, and implementation complexity.
Choose a tool after defining the threatNo technique removes governance obligations

This is a conceptual survey. Differential privacy provides a formal disclosure guarantee when implemented and accounted for correctly. Federated learning changes where raw data move. Synthetic data changes the released representation. Encrypted computation changes what processors can see.

Each technique has conditions and failure modes. Students do not need the mathematics here, but they should not treat the labels as automatic privacy certification.

Technical controls have boundaries

Privacy risk is reduced, not wished away.

EncryptionProtects data from some observersbut not an authorized harmful use
De-identificationReduces direct linkagebut auxiliary data can restore identity
Federated learningLimits raw central collectionbut model updates can still disclose information
Differential privacyBounds individual contributionbut only under the stated implementation and privacy budget
State what the control protects againstTest the implementation, not the label

Every control protects against a threat under assumptions. A privacy claim should name the adversary, protected information, mechanism, parameters, and residual risks.

NIST’s differential-privacy guidance emphasizes that poorly applied noise can either fail to protect privacy or make the data unusable.

Part II · Contestability

What happens when the data are wrong, the inference is disputed, or the decision should never have been automated?

An explanation is useful only if the institution can respond.

Privacy limits data powerContestability challenges its exercise

Transition from controlling information flows to responding after a system affects someone. The problem may be an incorrect record, a valid record used inappropriately, a model error, a policy disagreement, or a failure of process.

[Sources]
Alfrink et al., Contestable AI by Design: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-022-09611-z
Lyons, Velloso, and Miller, Perspectives on Contesting Algorithmic Decisions: https://doi.org/10.1145/3449180

Explanation is not contestability

“Here is why” is different from “you can change what happens next.”

EXPLAIN

Describe the decision

Show the role of the system, relevant evidence, policy, uncertainty, or important factors.

Outcome may remain fixed.
CONTEST

Open the decision to dispute

Accept corrections and new evidence, route review, reconsider the decision, and provide remedy.

Institution must be responsive.
Transparency can support contestationIt cannot substitute for it

A feature-importance chart may describe a model but offer no path to correction or remedy. Conversely, a useful appeal can sometimes succeed without revealing proprietary model details if it identifies the decisive evidence and policy.

Contestability is an organizational and procedural capability, not only an interface feature.

Five capabilities of a contestable system

People need more than a complaint box.

1NOTICEKnow that AI or data processing shaped the decision.
2ACCESSSee the relevant data, evidence, policy, and decision record.
3CORRECTFix inaccurate or mismatched information.
4CHALLENGEPresent reasons, context, and new evidence to a capable reviewer.
5REMEDYReceive a timely correction, reversal, explanation, or other repair.
A process is contestable when dispute can matterAuthority and deadlines are design requirements

This five-part sequence is a teaching synthesis grounded in contestable-AI research and procedural rights. An effective process must be discoverable, accessible, independent enough to judge the challenge, and authorized to change the outcome.

Measure completion time, reversal and correction rates, abandonment, accessibility, repeated error, and unequal use across groups.

Procedural requirement · Credit decisions

A lender cannot use model complexity as an excuse for vague denial reasons.

What was examined

CFPB guidance

The Bureau interpreted ECOA and Regulation B adverse-action requirements for creditors using complex algorithms and AI.

What was found

Specific principal reasons

Creditors must accurately identify the actual reasons for adverse action. A generic checklist or “the algorithm decided” is insufficient.

What it does not prove

The rule is sector-specific and does not require public disclosure of every model detail. It also does not guarantee that a consumer can easily reverse the result.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau · 2022 and 2023Complexity does not erase responsibility

The CFPB stated that creditors using complex algorithms must provide accurate, specific reasons for adverse action. Sample-form reasons cannot substitute for the factors actually used.

This is a useful design constraint: if the institution cannot generate a faithful case-level reason, the model may not fit the decision process.

Empirical case · Face surveillance

Rite Aid’s system generated thousands of false matches, while customers often had no notice or meaningful challenge.

What was examined

Retail face-recognition deployment

The FTC complaint examined enrollment images, match alerts, employee actions, safeguards, and demographic patterns.

What was found
5-year ban

The proposed order barred face surveillance, required biometric deletion, and mandated stronger safeguards for future automated systems.

What it does not prove

The allegations and order concern Rite Aid’s deployment and period of use. They do not establish that every facial-recognition implementation produces the same harms.

Federal Trade Commission · Rite Aid · 2023A false alert became a public accusation

The FTC alleged thousands of false-positive matches. Employees followed, searched, removed, or called police on customers. The system disproportionately affected people of color and was more likely to produce false positives in stores in plurality-Black and Asian communities than plurality-White communities.

The case links technical quality, data quality, notice, employee workflow, demographic impact, and inability to contest in the moment.

Scope, exceptions, and implementation matterThis slide is an overview, not legal advice

GDPR Articles 15, 16, and 22 support access, rectification, and safeguards for certain solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. Article 86 of the EU AI Act provides an explanation right for affected people in specified high-risk AI decisions with significant adverse impact.

The AI Act has phased application dates and amendments. Avoid presenting a universal right to every explanation or every model. The consolidated act dated July 27, 2026 states general application from August 2, 2026, while parts of the high-risk chapter have later dates.

Return to the opening case

A meaningful response requires more than revealing the model’s top factors.

PRIVACY RESPONSE

Identify every source and inference.

Test whether insurance use fits the collection purpose and context.

Limit retention, sharing, and future use.

CONTESTABILITY RESPONSE

Notify the person and provide the decisive reasons.

Allow data correction and relevant counter-evidence.

Route timely independent review with authority to reverse and repair.

The system is not accountable until someone can change the record, the decision, or the practice.
Inference creates obligationsExplanation should open a path to action

Resolve the fictional scenario. The institution should identify the data sources, inferred health claim, purpose, recipients, decision policy, and human roles. It should also provide a practical correction and appeal pathway.

A technically accurate inference can still be inappropriate to use. A lawful data source can still produce an incorrect match. Privacy, accuracy, fairness, and contestability are distinct checks.

Six questions to keep

Before an AI system acts on personal data, ask:

1 What did the system collect, acquire, derive, and infer?

2 Does each information flow fit the context and stated purpose?

3 What could an authorized user, partner, attacker, or model user learn?

4 Which data and retention can be removed?

5 Can a person see and correct the evidence that mattered?

6 Can a challenge change the decision and repair the harm?
